## Why the Standard Story Is Too Blunt

The massacre-first story is not merely common because it is possible but rather because it is narratively irresistible. It offers chiefs, banners, horses, firelight, screaming, trophies, and the great explanatory narcotic of visible violence. It lacks the modern explosions, but has plenty of mayhem. It flatters the historical imagination because it presents causality in a form that can be staged; Shakespeare's darker plays. One imagines the raid, the conquest, the broken walls, the smoking settlement, and so one has seen the common mechanism. Violent explanation enjoys a prestige wildly out of proportion to its actual analytic precision. It is said to be hard-headed, unsentimental, and serious. Quite often it is merely explanatory laziness wearing a helmet with Viking horns (which they did not).

I don't elide historical violence. Men on horseback did in fact set things on fire. Raiding, conquest, slavery, seizure of women, extinguished lineages - history offers an inventory. But the existence of violence does not grant it explanatory monopoly over every pattern that can be made to resemble aftermath. A bounded compression of paternal lines, especially one not mirrored in the maternal channel, is not automatically best understood as a saga of singular events using swords and salted domains. It may instead reflect a long, structured filtering of reproductive throughput: legitimacy, inheritance, prestige, exclusion, taboo, alliance, and the many other small gates through which societies quietly do their work. What is wanted here is not mere event-history, but instead control-system history.

That distinction matters because most people are educated to think historically largely as events. Battles, kings, migrations, collapses, treaties, decisive turns, and the scenery of consequence. The cultural machinery, when it appears at all, is treated as backdrop. But a great many historical outcomes are not event-produced in that theatrical sense. They are system-produced: the cumulative effect of repeated constraints, incentives, permissions, exclusions, and feedbacks operating over many generations. Once one sees the Y-bottleneck as a problem of lineage throughput rather than merely one of male mortality, the conventional taste for cavalry and bonfires begins to look less like realism than like a refusal to ask what sort of machinery could have been doing the work the whole time.

And that, to my eye, is a real paradigm shift. Once one admits the possibility of control-system history, battles no longer suffice. One needs multi-causal machinery. Models. Not because machines are magically objective, but because a runnable model will at least force the question into a form where rates, thresholds, channels, feedbacks, and topology have to declare themselves. Tunable knobs, with differing outcomes, testable against historical evidence. This is preferable to keep rewarding whatever story arrives wearing the loudest boots.
